Where the screening programme chokes

A block model of the whole screening service, from the camera to the ophthalmologist. Every stage is drawn with its capacity, the demand placed on it, and the ratio between them. The stage where that ratio is highest is the choke point — it, and nothing else, sets how many people the programme can actually screen.

This is a planning model, not the AI. Nothing on this page touches a fundus image or the classifier. It is the systems-engineering half of the problem: a Simulink-style block diagram of the screening service, solved in the browser so the numbers move while you change them. The intended production form of this analysis is a Simulink model in MATLAB; this is a faithful but lightweight stand-in, and its arithmetic is stated in full at the bottom of the page so it can be checked rather than trusted.

Signal-flow diagram

Each block is filled by its utilisation — demand divided by capacity. Wires carry the demand handed from one stage to the next, and thicken with it; where a stage cannot meet the demand on its wire, a red badge gives the daily shortfall. The choke point is drawn with a heavy outline and labelled, so it is identifiable without relying on colour. Everything updates as you change the parameters below.

    How each figure is worked out

    Nothing here is fitted or randomly simulated. Every capacity is one line of arithmetic on the inputs, so the model can be argued with directly.

    QuantityDefinition
    Patients per daypopulation per year ÷ 365.
    Usable images per daypatients per day × images per patient.
    Capture attempts per dayusable images ÷ (1 − reject rate). A rejected capture still occupies the camera, so the acquisition stage carries the retakes and no other stage does — the quality gate runs on the capture device, before upload.
    Acquisition capacitystations × images per station per day.
    Transfer capacitybandwidth (Mbps) × 3600 × transfer hours ÷ (image size in MB × 8). Protocol overhead and retransmission are not modelled, so this is an optimistic ceiling.
    Inference capacityimages per minute per node × 1440 × nodes.
    Review demandpatients per day × referral rate. Counted in patients, not images: a specialist reviews a person, both eyes at once.
    Review capacityophthalmologists × reviews per day each.
    Utilisationdemand ÷ capacity at that stage. Above 1.0 the stage cannot keep up and a queue forms behind it.
    Achievable screening ratetarget ÷ the highest utilisation, when that exceeds 1.0. A chain runs at the speed of its slowest stage, so one number — the worst utilisation — sets the whole programme's ceiling.

    What this model leaves out

    It is a steady-state capacity model, so it says nothing about variance: it assumes demand arrives evenly, that staff and cameras are available every day they are counted, and that no stage fails. Real queues are worse than steady-state ones, because arrivals bunch and servers go down — so wherever this model shows a stage running just under 100%, expect a queue in practice. It also treats specialist review as a single pool rather than a rota with leave and travel, ignores the time a patient waits between stages, and assumes every flagged patient is reviewed exactly once. Read it as a floor on the difficulty, not a forecast.
